Constraints, biases & investment decisions

01Constraints & choices

First identify the boundary. Then examine the decision.

An objective constraint changes what can be done. A bias changes how information or choices are evaluated. They can interact: an investor may have a genuine tax cost on a sale and also refuse to sell until the price returns to an arbitrary purchase level. Investigate both rather than treating the whole objection as irrational.

A constraint to respect

A planned medical payment requires accessible money by a fixed date. Portfolio design must account for it. Education about long-term returns cannot remove that cash need.

A pattern to investigate

“I cannot sell below what I paid” may signal anchoring or loss aversion. Ask about the goal, evidence, tax and alternatives before drawing a conclusion.

Behavioural finance studies how limited attention, shortcuts, emotions and social influences affect financial decisions. It complements portfolio theory by recognising that a technically efficient plan can fail if the client abandons it under pressure. Advisers and researchers have biases too.

Original practice: identify both elements

Sasha needs money for study in two years but wants to keep every inherited share because selling feels disloyal. The deadline and funding requirement are constraints. The attachment is a preference with emotional meaning; it may interact with endowment or familiarity effects. Clarify what preserving the inheritance means, quantify the funding risk and compare ways of honouring the intention.

A label is not a diagnosis.

Ask neutral questions and test the evidence. A legitimate values screen or a conservative preference is not automatically an error to correct. The goal is an informed, workable choice with the client.

02Three theory lenses

Each model answers a different question.

LensQuestion it helps answerLimit to explain
MPT: portfolio interactionHow do expected returns, risks and correlations combine across holdings?Forecast inputs and correlations are uncertain; the feasible set must reflect the client.
CAPM: market sensitivityWhat expected return does a model associate with this beta exposure?Beta omits company-specific and other factor risks; estimates can drift.
EMH: information in pricesHow difficult is it to earn persistent excess returns from available information after costs?Market efficiency varies; neither an active nor a passive label proves suitability.

The weak form of the efficient markets hypothesis concerns past market data; the semi-strong form concerns public information; the strong form concerns all information, including private information. These are progressively stronger hypotheses. They do not say that prices never move or that every observed price is the correct value.

A diversified, low-cost core can reduce dependence on selecting a winning manager or timing the market. A satellite is a limited additional allocation with a defined purpose and risk budget. Even a small satellite can contribute substantial risk if it overlaps the core, uses leverage or holds illiquid assets. Always examine the combined exposures.

Why “more funds” may not mean more diversification

Three funds can hold the same large growth companies. Compare their holdings and factor exposures, not just their different names. The same problem can arise if a salary, employer shares and home region all depend on one industry.

Market anomalies such as momentum, size or value effects are empirical patterns in specified samples. Their existence does not guarantee an exploitable future profit. A systematic momentum strategy with defined signals and controls is different from buying a popular asset because friends made money. Long-term reversal is a possibility to investigate, not a promise that every losing investment will recover.

Course coverage: §2.2, PDF p. 5; §3.1, pp. 8–9. EMH forms are an additional conceptual study aid.

03Tracking error

How variable is the gap from the benchmark?

An active return is the portfolio return minus the benchmark return for the same period and on the same basis. Tracking error is the standard deviation of those active returns. Tracking difference describes the performance gap over a stated period; it is not the dispersion of that gap.

Ex-post tracking error

Calculated from realised return observations. Specify frequency, sample window, fee basis, benchmark and annualisation convention.

Ex-ante tracking error

Estimated from current exposures and a risk model. It depends on assumptions about covariance and holdings; it can differ from future realised tracking error.

Sample tracking error = √[Σ(active return − mean active return)² ÷ (n − 1)]For equally spaced monthly observations, a common annualisation is monthly tracking error × √12. It assumes the scaling is appropriate; serial dependence can undermine it.

A 3% tracking error is not a ±3% promise.

It describes dispersion relative to the benchmark under a particular calculation or model. Outcomes can fall outside that range. Low tracking error does not prove quality, and high tracking error does not demonstrate skill.

04Recognise the pattern

Listen for the reference point behind the decision.

PatternA fictional cuePotential cost and useful response
Overconfidence“I can pick every turning point.”Excess turnover and concentration: compare after-cost results, use position limits and test contrary evidence.
Anchoring“My purchase price is the only fair value.”Delays a forward-looking decision: revisit current prospects, goals and opportunity cost.
Availability and recency“Yesterday’s headline means another crash is certain.”Overweights vivid or recent evidence: compare broader histories and plausible scenarios.
Loss aversion“Selling makes the loss real.”May prolong poor exposure or discourage necessary risk: discuss both short-term losses and long-term goal shortfall.
Mental accounting“The bonus is free money to gamble with.”Ignores whole-household risk: assess all resources and commitments together.
Herd behaviour“Everyone in my group has bought it.”Replaces independent assessment with social proof: return to evidence and the agreed policy.
Confirmation“Only send me reports that support my view.”Filters out disconfirming information: record a serious counterargument and test it.

The disposition effect is the tendency to sell winners while holding losers too long. It can involve loss aversion and anchoring, but actual tax costs and changing fundamentals still need examination. Mental buckets can be helpful for budgeting; the problem is failing to assess their combined economic consequences.

Original reflection: two reasons for staying in cash

One investor stays in cash because a house purchase is due soon; another has a flexible 25-year goal but reacts to a frightening headline. The same holding can arise from different circumstances. Ask about cash needs, capacity, understanding and goals before suggesting that either person change exposure.

Present gains and losses consistently. Explaining longevity or inflation risk should broaden understanding, not pressure the client into more risk than the plan can support.

Course coverage: §3.2–§3.4, PDF pp. 9–15. Confirmation bias is also linked to Topic 3.2.

05Stress tests & trade-offs

Turn a market story into a testable scenario.

A stress test applies specified shocks to a portfolio and asks what happens to value, cash access, spending and limits. It is a conditional illustration, not a prediction or a probability statement. Test several relevant threats: market falls, income interruption, wider spreads, restricted redemptions and values-aligned holdings lagging a broad index.

Back-test

Reconstructs how a rule would have behaved using past data. Check whether the rule, asset universe and data were actually available at the time, and include realistic costs.

Attribution

Examines the sources of observed results, such as asset allocation or selection. It helps explain what happened; it does not prove that the same source will persist.

Trade-off mapping compares feasible options under the same assumptions: expected return, downside, cash access, tax, fees, values fit and implementation effort. Avoid choosing a frightening scenario simply to steer a client toward a preferred product.

06Design the safeguards

A useful safeguard connects a pattern to an action.

Observed riskPossible agreed safeguardWhat still needs judgement
Impulsive tradingA reflection period and written reason for large discretionary changesUrgent liquidity or a genuinely changed investment case may require a different response.
Portfolio driftCalendar reviews and allocation bandsRebalancing costs, taxes, access and current suitability.
Repeated market timingRegular contributions and a documented investment scheduleAffordability and changing goals; averaging purchases does not guarantee profit.
Concentration or themesPosition and satellite limits with whole-portfolio exposure checksOverlap, leverage and risk contribution, not only dollars allocated.
Panic during withdrawalsCash policy, scenario discussion and a communication planThe reserve’s sustainability and the client’s updated circumstances.

Use a sequence: identify the cue, ask what is driving it, show the financial consequence, compare options, agree a proportionate safeguard and document the review process. Separate a goals conversation from a short-term performance discussion when that helps the client think clearly.

Original application: a popular theme has doubled

Before adding more, check its new portfolio weight, overlap with other holdings, downside exposure and purpose. Compare leaving it unchanged, trimming to the agreed range and adding to a different exposure. Record costs and tax. A rule is useful only if its rationale still fits; neither a recent gain nor a recent loss settles the decision.

Review the adviser’s own incentives and confidence. Ask what evidence would change the recommendation, whether a cheaper or simpler option meets the same need, and whether the client can explain the trade-off. Retain both the chosen option and the reasons alternatives were rejected.

Teach-back prompt

Explain why a cash buffer, a cooling-off period and a position cap solve different problems. Then describe one situation where each might be insufficient.
