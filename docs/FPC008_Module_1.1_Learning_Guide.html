01 The essentials

Turn a wish into something you can plan for.

Discovery combines financial facts with the meaning behind them. A preferred investment is a starting point for enquiry, not a complete objective.

Direction

Goal

The broad life outcome a client wants.
“I want more flexibility at work.”

A measurable target

Objective

An amount, timing and outcome.
“Build $45,000 by July 2029 to fund a year of reduced hours.”

A boundary

Constraint

A limit the plan must recognise.
“The emergency reserve must remain accessible.”

The examples throughout this guide are newly created for practice.

The missing fourth piece: priority.

Two sensible objectives may compete for the same money. Ask which matters most, what can move, and what cannot. Record the client’s reasons as well as the ranking.

What to leave discovery with

  • Goals in the client’s own language and measurable objectives.
  • Priority order and explicit trade-offs.
  • Cash-flow, liquidity, time, legal and behavioural constraints.
  • Separate findings on tolerance, capacity and risk need.
  • Evidence that important inconsistencies were tested.
Try explaining it

“I want a high-return fund.”

What will the money do? When will it be needed? What loss or delay would be unacceptable? Which other commitments compete with it?

Reveal the learning point

A product or return preference does not tell you the client’s underlying purpose, realistic funding requirement or ability to sustain losses. Discovery fills those gaps before strategy selection.

02 Discovery frameworks

Give the conversation a useful shape.

The module uses three complementary tools. Choose and adapt them to the person, language, cultural context and depth of enquiry needed.

From life aspirations to action

EVOKE

Explore the client’s situation, form a picture of their desired life, identify barriers, develop the knowledge needed and translate the discussion into action. The model is associated with George Kinder’s life-planning approach.

EExplorationWhat is happening in your life?
VVisionWhat would a fulfilling future look like?
OObstaclesWhat could get in the way?
KKnowledgeWhat information or options would help?
EExecutionWhat steps could make it happen?

“If work became optional, what would you want your week to look like?”

Watch for: a rigid sequence that feels scripted. Allow the client’s story to guide the pace.

Keep a record: the client’s vision, barriers, choices and how the eventual strategy responds.

From a surface preference to its meaning

5 Whys

Use successive, respectful follow-up questions to understand why a stated preference matters. Five is a prompt to explore depth, not a requirement to ask the same question exactly five times.

“What would owning that investment make possible for you?”

An illustrative chain: wanting a rental property → wanting dependable income → wanting less reliance on work → wanting time to care for a parent. The final insight is a hypothesis to confirm with the client.

Watch for: sounding like an interrogation, nudging the client towards your preferred answer, or accepting answers given to please you.

Keep a record: the original preference, useful probes and the meaning the client confirms. Do not invent a deeper motivation.

From life domains to financial priorities

Wheel of Life

Invite the client to consider satisfaction and desired change across relevant domains such as family, health, work, relationships and leisure. Ratings open a conversation; they do not determine an investment allocation.

“Which part of life would you most like to improve, and what would that require?”

Watch for: imposing categories, assuming a low rating means a financial problem, or relying on a visual format that does not suit the client.

Keep a record: the domains the client prioritises, the resulting goals and compromises they agree to.

Frameworks complement the numbers.

Insight into a client’s motivation does not replace cash-flow analysis, balance-sheet evidence or checking whether a plan is feasible.

03 Risk attitudes & preferences

Willing, able and required are different questions.

DimensionWhat it testsUseful evidence
Tolerance / attitudePsychological willingness to live with uncertainty and losses.Structured questionnaire, past responses, specific loss scenarios and the client’s explanation.
CapacityFinancial ability to absorb a loss without undermining essential commitments.Income stability, reserves, debts, near-term withdrawals, asset base and time horizon.
NeedThe return and associated risk implied by the objective and its funding gap.Goal amount, contributions, time available and reasonable modelling assumptions.

The module favours psychometrically designed questionnaires over ad-hoc questioning for consistent risk-tolerance assessment. A useful score still needs interpretation alongside the client’s circumstances, conversation and behaviour.

A high risk need is not permission to take high risk.

If a goal needs more risk than the client can bear or fund, revisit the goal, date, savings, spending or alternatives. A questionnaire score cannot resolve that conflict on its own.

Interactive / Make an abstract loss concrete

What does a fall actually mean?

Dollar loss$50,000
Balance after fall$200,000
Gain needed from the lower balance to recover25.0%

Arithmetic illustration, not a forecast. No contributions, withdrawals, tax or fees. Recovery gain = loss rate ÷ (1 − loss rate). This says nothing about how long recovery might take.

Ask two different follow-up questions

Tolerance

“How would you feel, and what might you do, if you saw that lower balance?”

Capacity

“Which bills, withdrawals or goals would be affected if the balance stayed lower for longer?”

04 Values & preferences

Ask what the label means to this client.

“Ethical”, “secure”, “family” and “financial freedom” can mean different things to different people. Turn broad preferences into clear, client-owned criteria.

Investment values

Three approaches to know

Negative screening: rule out specified activities or exposures.

Positive screening: favour investments that meet stated environmental, social or governance criteria.

Impact investing: pursue intentional, measurable social or environmental outcomes alongside a financial return.

Discovery questions

Make the preference usable

  • What must be included or excluded?
  • What thresholds and exceptions are acceptable?
  • What evidence or oversight would build confidence?
  • How would costs, diversification and different outcomes affect the preference?
  • How and when should alignment be reviewed?
A label is not proof of alignment.

Inspect the actual investment approach. An exclusion can change the investment universe, costs or concentration; it does not automatically mean lower returns. Discuss the specific trade-offs rather than assuming them.

Lifestyle, culture and relationships also shape a plan.

A career break changes the timing of cash needs. Support for relatives can affect buffers and priorities. Religious principles may shape permitted investments and the assurance a client wants. Cultural and kinship responsibilities should be explored in the client’s own terms, without assuming that everyone in a group has the same wishes.

Where a wish depends on legal rules, beneficiary eligibility or product terms, separate the intention from what can actually be implemented. Verify the relevant requirements and involve an appropriate specialist when needed. Agreement cannot make an invalid arrangement valid.

How would you record a preference well?

Illustrative record: “The client wants to avoid a specified activity, has explained why it matters, and will confirm the acceptable threshold after reviewing how different screens work. We still need to compare costs and diversification effects. No product choice has been agreed.”

This separates a confirmed value, an unresolved detail and the work still required.

05 Behaviour & bias

Treat a possible bias as a question to investigate.

Listen for ambiguity, missing information and the influence of recent events. An unusual preference can be entirely reasonable. Check the evidence before labelling it.

Confirmation bias

Pattern: giving more weight to evidence that supports an existing belief.

Original probe: “What evidence would make you reconsider that view?” Compare alternatives on the same criteria and time periods.

Status quo bias

Pattern: preferring the existing arrangement because change itself feels difficult.

Original probe: “What is valuable about keeping it, and what concerns you about changing?” Staying put may have sound cost, tax or insurance reasons.

Overconfidence

Pattern: overstating knowledge, control or certainty about future behaviour.

Original probe: “What could go differently, and how would you respond?” Use concrete scenarios and prior experience to test the claim.

Recency and loss aversion

Patterns: a recent event dominates expectations; a possible loss has greater emotional weight than a comparable gain.

Original probe: “Was this preference present before that event? Which outcome are you most concerned about?” Do not dismiss legitimate financial vulnerability as bias.

Framing, omissions and the illusion of transparency

The illusion of transparency is the mistaken belief that what we mean is clear to others. Different wording can change a response. A client may omit an important obligation or believe they have explained something that you understood differently.

Use balanced descriptions, ask what has not yet been discussed, and invite the client to explain the consequences in their own words.

Fear of missing out (FOMO)

Pattern: urgency to invest because other people or recent headlines appear to show gains.

Original probe: “If the recent gains had not happened, how would this investment serve your goals?” Test affordability, liquidity and alternatives before treating urgency as a sound reason to act.

Adviser projection and the curse of knowledge

Projection: your own investment preferences influence your interpretation of the client.

Curse of knowledge: you assume the client understands familiar concepts as you do. Compare your conclusion with the client’s actual evidence and use plain-language teach-back.

Probe → test → record → revisit.

A useful record explains the initial statement, relevant context, alternative explanations, the test used, the client’s response and what changed. A bias label alone explains very little.

Use behavioural supports carefully.

Balanced reframing can show a decision from another angle. Pre-agreed review and rebalancing rules can give a client a plan for stressful conditions. These supports must fit the client and allow reassessment when circumstances change; they do not replace ongoing consent or appropriate advice.

06 Put it into practice

Build a conclusion from more than one source.

Triangulation means comparing accounts, testing apparent contradictions and explaining how you reached a conclusion. It does not mean averaging conflicting scores.

  1. Compare. Put questionnaire results beside conversations, financial records and relevant past behaviour. Look for gaps or different meanings.
  2. Test. Ask neutral follow-ups and use specific scenarios. Check whether a contradiction reflects misunderstanding, changed circumstances or a real constraint.
  3. Document. Record what was resolved, what remains uncertain and why the resulting approach fits the client. Do not force a portfolio label before the evidence supports it.
S

Meet Sam

Original fictional case · Three discovery decisions

Sam, 43, wants the option to reduce work in three years. A questionnaire suggests comfort with substantial investment risk. Sam also says a large fall could lead them to sell, and has set aside $55,000 for the work transition. Essential spending and family support continue during that period.

Discussion path

1. Clarify Sam’s required amount, timing and competing priorities. 2. Separate long-term funds from the transition amount, then test dollar losses, liquidity and likely behaviour. 3. Record conflicting evidence and missing inputs, resolve them with Sam and compare feasible alternatives before selecting a strategy.

What would a useful file note contain?

  • The sources and dates of the evidence.
  • Sam’s objectives, priorities and financial boundaries.
  • Why the questionnaire and conversation differ.
  • Scenarios considered and Sam’s explanations.
  • Alternatives, trade-offs and demonstrated understanding.
  • Unresolved inputs and agreed review triggers.
Pause and retrieve

Why is “choose balanced” not enough?

Reveal a model explanation

A middle label does not reconcile the evidence or prove suitability. The relevant spending, investment horizon, liquidity requirements, capacity, tolerance and funding need still need to be understood. A recommendation needs a reasoned link to those circumstances.

This is a discovery exercise. It does not recommend a portfolio for Sam.

Official context and a precision note

The module links discovery to best interests, appropriateness and client understanding. The following official sources were checked on 14 October 2026:

  • ASIC RG 175: conduct and disclosure guidance, including the distinct duties to act in a client’s best interests and provide appropriate advice.
  • Corporations Act 2001: s961B concerns best interests and relevant circumstances; s961G separately concerns appropriateness. The s960A obligations cannot be contracted out of.
  • Financial Planners and Advisers Code of Ethics 2019: Standard 2 addresses honesty and best interests; Standard 4 addresses free, prior and informed consent; Standard 5 addresses individual appropriateness and reasonable grounds for believing the client understands benefits, costs and risks; Standard 6 concerns broader interests and longer-term effects.
  • ASIC INFO 271: sustainability terminology, investment approaches, screens and disclosure. The preference questions in this guide are a practical application, not a quotation of an adviser-specific rule.

Precision note: one module summary associates informed consent with Standard 5. The explicit consent standard is Standard 4; Standard 5 also requires appropriateness and client understanding. A signature or agreement does not by itself establish appropriate advice or remove statutory duties.

One-minute recap

Find the purpose. Make it measurable. Agree the priorities. Separate willingness from ability and need. Clarify values. Test conflicting evidence. Explain the conclusion and what would trigger a review.
