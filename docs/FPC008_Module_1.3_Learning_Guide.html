01Collect & reconcile

A number needs a source, a date and a meaning.

Module 1.1 explored the person; Module 1.2 made the reasoning traceable. This module tests what the financial evidence can actually support.

Collect the information relevant to the advice: income and spending, assets and debts, insurance, family commitments, goals, investment experience, risk willingness, loss capacity and constraints. A completed form is a starting point. Verify material inputs and reconcile information that does not agree.

Provenance

Where did it come from?

Record the owner, source document, statement date, valuation basis and reporting period. Distinguish a current statement from an estimate made during a meeting.

“Shares: $70,000, Alex’s portfolio statement as at 30 September” is more useful than “investments: $70,000”.

Reasonableness

Does it fit the other evidence?

Compare payslips with bank deposits, the spending estimate with transactions, and debts with lender statements. Check whether a figure is gross or net, monthly or annual, a balance or a payment.

An annual tax return can explain income history; it does not necessarily establish current take-home pay.

An evidence trail you can follow

  1. Collect. Ask for the relevant records and explain why they matter. Note missing documents.
  2. Standardise. Align dates, ownership and units. Annualise recurring cash flows while keeping one-off items separate.
  3. Reconcile. Compare independent sources; identify transfers, duplicates, timing differences and omissions.
  4. Resolve. Ask the client about discrepancies and seek corroborating evidence. Do not average incompatible numbers.
  5. Record. Retain the original estimate, verified result, explanation and impact on the analysis. Name who will resolve any remaining gap and when.
Try it: a client estimates spending of $4,000 a month, but bank debits average $5,500.

First establish what the debits contain. Transfers between the client’s accounts are not household consumption. Credit-card purchases and the later card repayment must not both be counted as spending. Separate loan payments, investment contributions, unusual bills and actual living expenses. Then compare like with like and discuss any remaining difference.

Neither the original estimate nor the bank total should automatically replace the other. Record the reconciled categories and the period reviewed; include enough history to catch annual and seasonal costs.

Conflict is a question to investigate.

A wish for capital certainty alongside a high return target may be incompatible. Explain the trade-off and revisit the goal, deadline or funding. Likewise, a short goal deadline does not make preserved super available. Agreement with a warning is not a substitute for resolving material gaps.

02Cash flow & uncertainty

Measure the surplus. Then challenge it.

Cash flow describes movement over a period. It helps reveal whether ongoing commitments and planned saving are affordable.

Net cash surplus= cash inflows − cash outflows, for the same period

Choose a consistent method. Start with gross cash income and deduct the relevant cash tax payments, or start with take-home cash income and avoid deducting that same tax again. A tax calculation is a separate reconciliation: taxable income and money received are not interchangeable.

Recurring cash flows

Include reliable income, living costs, debt service, insurance and other commitments. Convert weekly × 52, fortnightly × 26 and monthly × 12 for a standard annual estimate; check actual pay dates for precise forecasts.

Example: $250 a week is $13,000 a year, or about $1,083 a month—not $1,000.

One-offs and changes

Show a bonus, inheritance, asset sale or renovation separately. Label uncertainty and timing. Model parental leave, childcare, a job change or retirement in the period in which cash flows change.

A once-only receipt does not establish a sustainable annual saving rate.

Precision check: cash, tax and investment contributions

A franking credit is a tax credit, not extra cash arriving with the dividend. A reinvested dividend may be taxable income without providing cash to pay living costs. Reconcile cash received, reinvestment and any tax payment or refund separately. Moneysmart explains investment tax and franking credits.

The full principal-and-interest loan payment leaves the bank account; principal repayment also reduces the liability. Employer super contributions are not take-home spending money. A transfer into investments is a use of surplus: make clear whether the reported surplus is before or after planned saving.

Original fictional household · Noor & Alex

A positive annual figure can hide a fragile plan.

Monthly take-home income is $7,800. Essentials cost $3,100, debt payments $2,200 and discretionary spending $700. Other recurring annual bills total $9,600 and are not included in those monthly amounts.

Base case: $93,600 income − $81,600 outgoings = $12,000 annual surplus, equivalent to $1,000 a month before new saving commitments.

Downside: reduce income by 10% and raise essentials by 10%, holding other items fixed. The surplus becomes a $90 monthly deficit. A positive base case alone is not evidence that a new commitment is resilient.

Cash-flow stress lab

All inputs are fictional AUD cash amounts. Income is already after tax. Annual bills are additional to monthly costs. The stress applies for 12 months; debt payments, discretionary spending and annual bills stay fixed. Inputs reset when you reopen the guide.

This is a sensitivity illustration, not a forecast, tax model or borrowing assessment. It excludes returns and changes beyond the selected shocks. Annual averages do not show whether cash is available on a particular bill’s due date.

Make uncertainty useful

Keep a base case and plausible downside cases. Test income interruptions, expense increases, changed debt costs and large bills. Use a month-by-month view where timing matters. Record each assumption’s source, date and reason; identify which action would be reviewed if the scenario occurred.

Reflection: the household still wants to invest $1,000 every month.

Explore the ability to reduce discretionary spending, stage contributions, change the target or extend the timeframe. Check accessible reserves and other commitments. A possible review trigger is an agreed fall in verified surplus for a specified period, followed by a named person reviewing contributions. Choose the threshold with the client; do not treat the example as an instruction.

Course coverage: §3.2, PDF pp. 5–8. Cash-versus-tax clarification and the fictional lab are additional study aids. The course’s tax illustration uses 2025–26 assumptions; this guide does not reproduce it or apply those figures to another tax year.

03Wealth & liquidity

Wealth on a date is different from cash you can use.

A balance sheet measures assets and liabilities at a point in time. Cash flow measures movement over time. Read them together.

Net worth = total assets − total liabilitiesCurrent values, a stated date and a clearly defined ownership boundary.

List lifestyle assets, investment assets and super separately. Identify legal ownership and shared interests, and avoid counting a jointly owned asset twice in a household total. Record loan balances separately from repayments. If an asset is omitted, label the result as a partial position rather than the client’s complete net worth.

Cash availability

What is uncommitted?

Of the $30,000 bank savings, suppose $4,000 is needed for the card, $18,000 is the agreed reserve and $3,000 is committed to a separate upcoming cost. That leaves $5,000 for further consideration.

This is an illustrative allocation, not a recommended reserve or permission to invest. Verify that commitments are complete and not deducted twice.

Short-term position

Net working capital

Current assets − current liabilities. Define the period, normally the next 12 months, and classify on that basis. Include the current portion of longer-term debt, not automatically its entire balance.

Illustration: $24,000 current assets − $17,000 current liabilities = $7,000. Positive working capital can still hide a timing gap, earmarked money or an asset that must be sold at an uncertain price.

Check the distinction: offset cash versus redraw

An offset balance is money in a separate linked account; additional repayments in redraw have already reduced the loan balance and access depends on the loan terms. In a gross balance sheet, show offset cash as an asset and the loan as a liability. Do not also subtract the same offset from the loan balance—that would count the benefit twice. Moneysmart: offset and redraw explained.

Review existing investments before proposing replacements.

Assess their role, risks, income, costs and suitability against the client’s goals. A disappointing return alone is not enough to justify a switch. Consider keeping the holding, the quality of the comparison, tax effects, transaction costs and any lost benefits. Document why the proposed change improves the client’s position.

Course coverage: §3.3, PDF pp. 8–9, and net working capital in §3.4, PDF p. 10. The statement, liquidity examples and offset check are original additions.

04Scope & allocation

Define what the numbers include.

An allocation percentage has meaning only when you know its denominator. An advice boundary has meaning only when its consequences are understood.

Prepare an asset-allocation view

Group holdings by their underlying exposures, total the portfolio being analysed, then divide each asset-class value by that total. Show the valuation date and exclusions. Separate current allocation from a proposed target and any permitted ranges.

50%30%20%
Shares: $100,000Fixed interest: $60,000Cash: $40,000

The shares weight is $100,000 ÷ $200,000 = 50%. This is an arithmetic example, not a suggested portfolio. It excludes a separate home, super and emergency reserve.

Look through a diversified fund or super investment option to its underlying assets when the data is available. “Super” describes a structure, not one asset class. Include a fund’s total once: do not also add its underlying holdings to the total. Assess concentration within classes and across the household as well as within the advice portfolio.

Current position

What does the client already hold? What risks, liquidity restrictions and concentrations are present? Does an allocation label conceal significant differences between products?

Proposed benchmark

Does the mix fit verified needs, willingness and capacity? Explain any difference from a relevant benchmark. Profile labels and asset-class ranges vary; “balanced” is not one universal allocation.

Limited advice still needs relevant context

A client can seek advice on a particular issue. Agree the subject and boundaries, but assess the relevant wider circumstances. An excluded property, debt or super account may still affect liquidity, concentration or the ability to meet the goal. Explain material limits and reconsider the scope if suitable advice cannot be given within it.

Select a scenario to reveal the reasoning.

Potential funding can come from existing accessible capital, sustainable future surplus or borrowing. These have different risks and constraints. Borrowed money creates a liability; it does not create net wealth on receipt. Super is usable only when the applicable access conditions permit it.

Course coverage: §§3.3–3.4, PDF pp. 8–10. Scope clarification: ASIC’s tips for giving limited advice, checked 26 October 2026.

05Life stages & capacity

Use life stage to ask better questions.

Life stages help identify likely changes in earnings, responsibilities and withdrawals. They do not determine a person’s risk profile.

Read the course’s age patterns as generalisations.

Age alone does not establish willingness, financial capacity or an appropriate asset allocation. A young person can have low loss capacity for a near-term goal. A retiree can have a long investment horizon for some money. Investigate the actual goal, resources, access and flexibility.

Ability and willingness answer different questions

Ability / capacity

Could the plan survive the loss?

Test spending relative to available assets, essential goals, liquidity, time horizons, income stability and flexibility. “We can wait” needs evidence that bills can still be paid while waiting.

Willingness / tolerance

Could the person stay with the plan?

Explore reactions to dollar losses, past behaviour, experience and understanding. Use a questionnaire as one input alongside the conversation and financial evidence.

A small sequencing example: same returns, different order

Start with $100,000 and withdraw $10,000 at the end of each of two years. Ignore tax, fees and inflation.

−20%, then +20%: year one ends at $70,000; year two ends at $74,000.
+20%, then −20%: year one ends at $110,000; year two ends at $78,000.

Both sequences contain the same two returns. The withdrawal timing changes the ending balance. Without withdrawals, both end at $96,000. This illustrates sequencing risk; it does not predict returns or prescribe a withdrawal rate.

06Goals & objectives

Connect what matters to what the money must do.

“Build wealth” needs more detail. Agree the purpose, amount, deadline, priority, current funding, acceptable flexibility and the consequences of falling short.

The course groups goals broadly into short term (the next two years), medium term (roughly two to seven years) and long term (beyond that, often retirement). These are organising conventions. Record the actual date and cash-flow pattern instead of relying on a label.

Original goal refinement

From “we want more freedom” to a testable goal

“Keep $18,000 available as our agreed reserve and build a separate $24,000 study-leave fund over 36 months. We have $6,000 already allocated. We can change the leave date, but we do not want to fund it with credit-card debt.”

Ignoring returns, fees, tax and inflation, the gap is $18,000: $500 a month for 36 months. Compare this commitment with the verified surplus and downside scenarios. The household may have several goals competing for that same surplus.

Return objective

What result is needed?

Separate the return needed to fund high-priority goals from a desired extra outcome. State the horizon, whether the figure is nominal or after inflation, and whether tax and fees are included.

A required return is an input to feasibility testing. It is not a promise that markets will deliver it.

Risk objective

What uncertainty is acceptable?

Combine the client’s willingness to accept losses with the plan’s ability to absorb them. Describe the consequences in dollars and goals, alongside relevant investment risks and liquidity constraints.

If the return need exceeds what a suitable risk level can reasonably support, revisit funding, spending, timing or priorities.

Income, growth and total return

Total return combines income and changes in asset value. A high cash yield alone does not establish a good overall outcome. A total-return approach can support spending through income and asset sales, but sales, market falls, settlement, tax, costs and cash buffers still matter. Unrealised growth is not money already available in a bank account.

Nominal-to-real return lab

Explore purchasing power over one period. Enter percentages on a consistent basis. This illustration ignores tax and fees; it is not an expected-return estimate.

Exact real return = (1 + nominal return) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1. Use decimals in the formula.

Exact real return2.91%

Why “6% return minus 3% inflation = 3% real” is only an approximation

$100 grows to $106. The basket that cost $100 now costs $103. The purchasing-power increase is $106 ÷ $103 − 1 = about 2.91%. Subtraction is a useful rough comparison at small rates; the ratio is the exact one-period calculation.

Resolve the plan before selecting the product.

Record the agreed priorities, verified resources, cash needs, objective definitions, risk evidence, remaining gaps and review triggers. Confirm the client’s understanding. If the goals cannot be achieved together, make the choices explicit and document the agreed adjustment.
