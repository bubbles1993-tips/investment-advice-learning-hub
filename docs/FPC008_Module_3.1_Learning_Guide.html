Performance & risk metrics

Measure returns on a fair basis, identify the risks behind them and explain what the numbers can—and cannot—tell you.

01Define the comparison

Before comparing returns, compare the definitions.

Absolute return describes the investment’s gain or loss over a period. Relative return compares it with a reference. A positive absolute return can lag its benchmark; a negative return can beat a falling benchmark. Neither statement alone establishes whether the client’s goal was advanced.

CheckWhy it matters
Dates and frequencyCompare the same start/end points and observation intervals; a one-year result is not a five-year annualised result.
Total return versus priceInclude distributions consistently and state whether they are reinvested.
Currency and hedgingAUD and foreign-currency results differ; hedged and unhedged series are different exposures.
Fees, costs and taxIdentify what is included in “net”. Platform, adviser, investor-specific tax or transaction costs may sit outside the published return.
Cash flowsDeposits and withdrawals change balances without being investment returns. Separate manager performance from the investor’s experience.
One-period total return = (ending value − starting value + income) ÷ starting valueAssume no external contributions or withdrawals and that ending value excludes separately paid income. $100 grows to $104 and pays $3: the total return is 7%.

Time-weighted returns chain subperiod investment returns to limit the effect of external cash-flow timing. Money-weighted returns reflect the amount and timing of cash flows, often using an internal rate of return. They answer different questions. A balance growing from $100,000 to $130,000 after a $25,000 contribution did not necessarily earn 30%.

Compounding check: +10% followed by −10%

A $100 investment becomes $110 then $99: a cumulative return of −1%. The arithmetic average is zero, while the two-period geometric average is √0.99 − 1 ≈ −0.50% per period. An arithmetic average and compound growth are not interchangeable.

Write the comparison in a sentence before calculating: “AUD total returns, monthly observations over the same five years, with the same fee treatment, against the documented benchmark.” This makes later ratios interpretable.

02Volatility & drawdown

Risk has more than one dimension.

Standard deviation

Measures dispersion around a mean, counting upside and downside deviations. Specify sample, frequency and annualisation. Smooth reported prices can understate the economic risk of illiquid assets.

Correlation

Measures the direction and strength of linear co-movement. Low historical correlation can help diversification but may change in stress. Zero correlation does not establish independence or absence of risk.

Beta is sensitivity to a chosen market: covariance with market returns divided by market variance. It is not the same as total volatility. A portfolio can have modest market beta and still carry substantial credit, liquidity, leverage or company-specific risk.

Drawdown at time t = current cumulative value ÷ highest prior cumulative value − 1Maximum drawdown is the deepest observed decline in that window. Use a total-return wealth series without external cash-flow distortions. A future decline may be larger.

Measure the experience as well as the return.

Compare depth and duration of losses with spending flexibility and loss capacity. Standard deviation alone does not describe tail losses, time underwater or the consequences of withdrawals.

03Risk-adjusted returns

Use the denominator that matches the question.

MeasureFormulaInterpretation
Sharpe ratio(portfolio return − risk-free return) ÷ portfolio volatilityExcess return per unit of total volatility; compare consistent periods and methods.
Information ratioMean active return ÷ tracking errorAverage return above the benchmark per unit of variability in that active return.
Jensen’s alphaPortfolio return − [risk-free return + beta × (market return − risk-free return)]Return not explained by a single-market CAPM comparison; not automatic proof of skill.

For a historical Sharpe ratio, calculate the mean excess return and its standard deviation from the return series. When the risk-free return is treated as constant, excess-return volatility equals portfolio volatility. Annualising an arithmetic monthly mean by 12 and standard deviation by √12 is a convention with assumptions. Do not quietly combine CAGR, monthly risk and annual rates.

When does the ranking mislead?

A higher positive Sharpe ratio may reflect a favourable sample, smoothed valuations or unmeasured tail risk. Negative Sharpe ratios are especially awkward for rankings because increasing volatility can make a negative ratio less negative. A zero denominator makes the ratio undefined. Compare similar mandates and examine the return distribution, not just the headline score.

Sharpe’s own discussion emphasises differential returns, time aggregation and the limits of using a single ratio. William Sharpe: The Sharpe Ratio is a useful primary reference. Downside measures such as Sortino use a different risk denominator and require a stated target and convention.

04Style & factor exposure

Apparent skill may be an exposure in disguise.

Style labels describe broad characteristics, such as growth or value. Factors are systematic drivers measured using specified data and models. Market beta, size, value, momentum and profitability can explain common return patterns. A manager’s residual alpha depends on which model is used.

ExposureWhat it commonly describesRisk to investigate
ValueLower prices relative to measures of fundamentalsA cheap business may be deteriorating; a value premium can fail to appear for long periods.
GrowthHigher valuations associated with expected expansionExpectations may disappoint or valuation multiples compress.
SizeExposure to smaller rather than larger companiesTrading liquidity, business risk and capacity; definitions differ by market and index.
MomentumA systematic tilt toward recent relative winnersTurnover, costs, crowding and abrupt reversals.
Quality/profitabilitySpecified measures of business resilience or profitabilityDifferent definitions can produce different portfolios and valuations.

Holdings analysis reveals what is owned now. Return-based regression estimates how past returns moved with selected factors. Attribution decomposes performance according to a stated framework, such as allocation and selection effects. Use them together: stale holdings or a short regression window can each miss a changing strategy.

Original example: three funds, one dominant driver

A portfolio owns three global equity funds. Their names and managers differ, but all favour large growth companies. Sector labels alone may conceal overlapping holdings and valuation sensitivity. Compare positions, factor estimates and downside scenarios before concluding that the manager count creates meaningful diversification.

Style rotation means the market’s relative winners change. A fund can follow its process and still lag when its style is out of favour. Conversely, strong performance in a favourable regime may not demonstrate stock-selection skill. Explain the exposure and the conditions in which it could struggle; avoid predicting the next style winner.

Course coverage: §1.3, PDF pp. 9–15.

05Benchmarks & evidence

A fair comparator is part of the analysis.

Choose the benchmark to fit the mandate before observing which index makes results look best. Consider asset class, region, size, style, currency, hedging, reinvestment and rebalancing. A multi-asset portfolio may need a documented composite rather than one equity index. A peer group is not automatically an investable, unbiased benchmark.

Evidence issueWhat to ask
SurvivorshipAre failed or merged funds included, or only the survivors?
Selection and backfillWere successful records added later? Was the start date chosen after the result was known?
PersistenceDid prior winners continue to win in later, non-overlapping periods?
Benchmark changesWere reasons, dates and treatment of the earlier history disclosed?
Net returnsWhich fees, costs and taxes are included, and are both sides comparable?

SPIVA compares active funds with stated category benchmarks and includes methodology designed to address issues such as survivorship. Read the region, category, horizon and report date before quoting a percentage. Aggregate underperformance does not prove that every active fund will lose, nor that the future winner can be selected in advance. S&P’s Australia Year-End 2025 scorecard provides a primary-source example; the course discusses earlier 2025 data.

The course also discusses investor-behaviour research such as DALBAR. Distinguish a fund’s time-weighted record from an investor experience affected by cash-flow timing. An observed gap does not, by itself, prove a single psychological cause or establish that one country’s aggregate result applies to every client.

Original benchmark test

A small-company strategy is compared only with a large-company index after small caps had a strong year. First investigate whether the index represents the strategy’s opportunity set. Retain any original benchmark history transparently, add a suitable comparator and explain why the conclusion changes. Do not erase an inconvenient old benchmark.

06Illiquidity & judgement

A neat statistic can rest on weak evidence.

Private-market investments often have irregular capital calls, distributions and infrequent valuations. A standard volatility comparison may be misleading if reported valuations adjust slowly. A high Sharpe ratio based on smooth marks does not establish that economic risk is low.

PME

Public Market Equivalent methods compare private-investment cash flows with a public index using matching cash-flow timing. Kaplan–Schoar PME includes distributions and any residual value relative to contributions, all scaled consistently to the index. A ratio above one indicates outperformance on that method, not necessarily after adjusting for all risk.

GPME

Generalised PME incorporates an asset-pricing model to account for systematic risk. Its interpretation depends on the exact method and normalisation; do not automatically apply the simple KS-PME “above one” rule. Model choice, cash-flow quality and residual valuations matter.

The course points to research seeking better private-market performance measures. The practical lesson is to identify the method and assumptions before interpreting the result. Do not compare an illiquid fund’s IRR directly with a public index’s CAGR and call the difference manager alpha.

Before relying on a metricUseful challenge
Return shapeWhat are the deepest losses, tail exposures and recovery periods?
RegimeDid the result depend on one interest-rate environment, market style or liquidity condition?
ModelDoes the residual change when relevant factors are included? Are estimates statistically uncertain?
Portfolio roleDoes the holding fit the client’s cash needs, capacity, costs and combined exposures?
Evidence qualityCan the result be reproduced from dated inputs and a documented method?
Original synthesis exercise

Fund Cedar has the higher recent return and Sharpe ratio, but values private holdings quarterly and restricts redemptions. Fund Willow has lower headline results and daily traded holdings. Before ranking them, investigate valuation smoothing, mandate, fees, cash-flow timing, drawdowns and withdrawal terms. They may serve different purposes; the metric alone cannot choose for the client.

Explain the conclusion in three sentences.

State what was measured and over which period. Name the main return driver and material limitation. Then explain why the finding does or does not help this client’s objective. Topic 3.2 takes the next step: checking the evidence behind the finding.
