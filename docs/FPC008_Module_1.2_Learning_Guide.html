01Conversation to evidence

Carry the “why” into the file.

Module 1.1 explored goals, values, constraints and risk. This module turns those insights into a record that can guide advice, implementation and later review.

  1. Client meaning. Capture the outcome in the client’s own terms and check your interpretation.
  2. Measurable objective. Agree the amount, timing, priority and boundaries. Keep unknowns visible.
  3. Evidence and testing. Connect the conversation to financial information, scenarios and any inconsistencies.
  4. Reasoned choice. Explain the alternatives, consequences and reasons for selecting or deferring a course of action.
  5. Understanding and follow-through. Record what the client understood, what they agreed to, who will act, and what will trigger a review.
A practical test

Could another adviser understand this decision without asking you to reconstruct the meeting from memory?

Weak record

“Client wants growth.”

This gives no purpose, deadline, liquidity boundary, evidence of loss capacity, or reason for the proposed strategy.

Better starting point

“Fund a planned work break.”

Record the required amount and date, the separate emergency reserve, the verified savings capacity, and what the client would change if the plan falls short.

02Choose the right tool

Choose for the question you need to answer.

A worksheet is useful when it reveals something that changes the conversation. Explain why you chose it, adapt it to the person and record what it revealed.

Unclear motivation

Explore the meaning

Use gentle “why” questions or a broad life-priorities discussion when the client has a product preference but no clear purpose.

Original prompt: “What would having this money let you do differently?”

Competing priorities

Make the trade-off concrete

Use a limited budget or priority-ranking exercise when the client wants several outcomes that compete for the same resources.

Original prompt: “If we can fully fund only one of these this year, which comes first?”

Purpose and legacy

Map people, intentions and boundaries

A values discussion and stakeholder map can clarify who benefits, who decides and how lifestyle needs limit family support or giving.

Original prompt: “What support feels right, and what needs to stay available for you?”

Conflicting evidence

Test a specific scenario

Compare questionnaire results with a dollar-loss example, cash needs and past behaviour. Record the explanation for any mismatch.

Original prompt: “If the balance fell while this expense was due, what would you do?”

Practice: a digital survey says “high risk”, but the money is needed next year.

Think first: what does the survey establish, and what is still missing?

The survey is one input. Clarify the goal’s deadline and flexibility, test the financial consequences of a loss, explore the response in the client’s own words, and document the conflict. An enthusiastic response does not establish financial capacity.

Practice: the client gives brief, agreeable answers to every question.

Check comprehension, language, pace and comfort with disagreement. Use neutral alternatives and allow time to reflect. Avoid diagnosing a bias from one interaction or assuming agreement confirms understanding.

Adaptation belongs in the record.

Note any interpreter or support person, agreed privacy boundaries, simplified language, shorter sessions or follow-up questions. Confirm individual preferences rather than inferring them from age, wealth or culture.

03Build a useful record

Separate facts, assumptions and judgements.

Use a consistent structure, then fill it with client-specific evidence. Reusable headings help; generic conclusions conceal the work.

An original study scaffold for a discovery file note
RecordWhat makes it useful?
Context and scopeDate, participants, agreed purpose, issues included and exclusions that need follow-up.
Goals and prioritiesThe client’s meaning, quantified objective, timing, ranking and any non-negotiable boundary.
EvidenceSource and date of figures; what the client reported; what has been checked; contradictions and missing information.
Assumptions and scenariosWhat was assumed, why, what changes under a downside scenario, and where the assumption needs verification.
Options and trade-offsRealistic alternatives, including keeping the current arrangement where relevant; material benefits, costs, risks and rejected choices.
ReasoningHow the evidence leads to the proposed next step; why alternatives fit less well; any issue preventing a conclusion.
Understanding and consentQuestions, explanations, the client’s response and the precise action authorised. Keep proposed actions separate from agreed ones.
Actions and reviewOwner, due date, required evidence and a dated or event-based trigger to revisit the decision.
Original fictional example · Morgan

A $30,000 work-break fund in 24 months

Morgan has $6,000 earmarked for the goal and reports being able to save $900 a month. The emergency reserve is separate. The budget and leave arrangements still need checking.

Reveal a stronger discovery note

Purpose and boundary: Morgan wants time away from work in 24 months without drawing on the emergency reserve.

Evidence status: $6,000 is reported as allocated to the goal; $900 monthly surplus is an estimate pending budget verification. Employer approval and actual leave costs remain open.

Scenario: Ignoring returns, fees, tax and changing costs, the estimated funding reaches $27,600. The $2,400 gap can be closed by an additional $100 a month, a smaller goal, a later date, or a combination.

Discussion: Explore the feasibility and consequences of those choices. Record Morgan’s actual response after discussion; do not pre-fill consent. Taking more investment risk is not an automatic solution to a shortfall.

Next step: Verify the surplus and costs, then confirm the goal and preferred trade-off before preparing a recommendation. Revisit if leave timing, earnings or the reserve requirement changes.

Goal-to-evidence lab

Change one assumption. Then explain what the file note needs to say. This is simple savings arithmetic, not an investment forecast.

Projected savings only—
Shortfall—
Monthly saving needed to meet the goal—

Calculation: allocated amount + monthly saving × months. No investment return, inflation, fees, tax, withdrawals or uncertain cash flows are modelled.

04SOA, ROA & file notes

Choose the document for the circumstances.

Statement of Advice

SOA

Personal advice to a retail client generally requires an SOA unless an exception applies. It explains the advice, its basis and the required disclosures.

Record of Advice

ROA

Available in specified situations. Existing-client status alone does not establish eligibility for the further-advice exception.

Supporting evidence

File notes

Capture the enquiries, discussions and reasoning. Supporting notes do not replace an SOA where one is required.

For the further-advice pathway

Check the earlier SOA and providing entity, changes in relevant circumstances and changes in the advice basis. Significant differences can prevent using this exception. Document your assessment and check all applicable conditions.

This is one pathway. Other exceptions have their own requirements. See ASIC’s ROA guidance.

The 2024 case: what the regulator actually found

ASIC reported that the FSCP suspended Ian Reid’s registration for three months from 21 November 2024. The further-advice exception did not apply to two clients because their relevant circumstances had changed significantly. For all three clients, the panel found insufficient evidence of compliant enquiries and advice reasoning under s961B and s961G.

The lesson is to reassess the client and demonstrate why earlier recommendations remain appropriate. It is not a rule that every ROA is inadequate, or that an SOA expires after a fixed number of years.

ASIC 24-258MR, 22 November 2024. Historical finding, not a statement about current registration.

05Ethics & understanding

Make agreement meaningful.

A signature is evidence of an action. Your record should also explain how you checked understanding of the decision and its consequences.

Explain and test

Invite a teach-back

“How would you describe the main trade-off in your own words?” Use the answer to identify misunderstanding, explain again and record what was clarified.

Respect the choice

Be specific about consent

Distinguish understanding, preference, agreement to advice and authority to implement. A note saying “happy to proceed” may leave the scope unclear.

Code of Ethics: distinctions to remember

StandardStudy focus
2Integrity and the client’s best interests.
4Free, prior and informed consent to acting for the client.
5Appropriate advice and reasonable grounds for being satisfied the client understands it, including product benefits, costs and risks.
6Broader effects and the client’s longer-term interests and likely circumstances.
7Consent to benefits and fees, restrictions on third-party benefits, and fairness, reasonableness and value.
8Complete and accurate client records.

Condensed study summaries of selected standards, not the full Code. Read the in-force Code.

Two precision checks

Standard 7 is specifically about benefits and fees; “clear disclosure” alone is an incomplete description. Standard 8 directly addresses records.

A sustainability preference does not inherently mean lower returns. Record the actual constraints, evidence, costs and scenario assumptions instead of asserting a universal performance trade-off.

Practice: the client agrees to a restriction that narrows investment choices.

Clarify exactly what is excluded and why it matters. Compare feasible choices, costs and diversification implications. Explain material uncertainty, check understanding, record the client’s priorities and identify when to revisit them. A preference or consent does not remove the adviser’s duties.

06Review & reflect

Build a record that can be revisited.

Review the reasoning as well as the balance. Check whether the goals, constraints, assumptions and client preferences still support the strategy.

Before advice progresses

Use a decision gate

  • Are material facts current and verified?
  • Are conflicts and missing inputs resolved or clearly escalated?
  • Have realistic alternatives and consequences been evaluated?
  • Is the document pathway justified?
  • Is there evidence of understanding and the necessary consent?
After implementation

Close the loop

  • Confirm what actually occurred and any departures from the plan.
  • Compare outcomes with the assumptions that informed the advice.
  • Check whether client priorities or constraints changed.
  • Record the owner and timing of the next action.
  • Update the process when recurring gaps become apparent.

Make review triggers actionable

VagueMore useful
“Review annually.”Set the review date, what evidence will be refreshed, who requests it and what will be reassessed.
“Contact us if things change.”Name relevant events: a work-break date moves, income changes, health affects work, or family-support commitments increase.
“Watch market volatility.”Agree the circumstances that prompt a discussion and the checks to make. A trigger prompts review; it is not automatically a trade instruction.
Reflection exercise: improve one part of a file.

Identify a judgement that is hard to reconstruct. Was the missing piece the evidence, an assumption, the alternative considered, the client’s response or the follow-up? Make one concrete process change, such as an evidence-date field or a peer check at a decision gate. Later, check whether it improved the next file.

Efficiency that preserves judgement

Use consistent headings, linked source records, clear version dates and targeted peer review. Remove duplicated administration, while keeping the evidence that explains the individual decision.
